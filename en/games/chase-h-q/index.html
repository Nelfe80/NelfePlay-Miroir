The story of the game

The why and the how

Chase H.Q. reached arcades in the autumn of 1988 from Taito, which developed and published it. The idea goes back to 1986: while Full Throttle was being made, Taito America asked for a driving game where two players face each other. The team tried 2D, 3D, then a mix of both, before settling in 1987 on a single-player 3D game, then titled CHASE. In the meantime Sega had launched Out Run, and Taito's cabinet steered away from it: the point was no longer to finish a course but to catch a fleeing criminal. The public followed. In 1989 the game became Japan's highest-grossing dedicated arcade machine and, in the United Kingdom, for a while it was the top coin-taking arcade game.

How it was made

Game design fell to Hiroyuki Sakou, who had already worked on Full Throttle and Operation Thunderbolt, at Taito's Osaka laboratory. He drew on Miami Vice, of which he is a fan: a pair of cops, one white and one black, scenery evoking America, and a supercar as the hero's ride. The car deliberately recalls the Porsche 928, to avoid comparison with Out Run, and the radio messages before each level owe something to Konami's Metal Gear. Takeshi Ishizashi, Takeshi Murata and Kyoji Shimamoto programmed the game. The music went to Takami Asano, guitarist of the band Godiego, whose work on a guitar-playing robot for Taito had put him on the company's path.

What makes it unique

At the wheel of a black Porsche 928, Tony Gibson and his partner Raymond Broady get a radio call from Nancy at headquarters before every level: a fugitive to find, and the car he is driving. Five levels, five criminals, from Ralph, the Idaho Slasher, in his white Lotus Esprit, to an Eastern Bloc spy in a red Porsche 928. A wheel, two pedals and a two-speed shifter control the car; a button on the shift knob fires a turbo, in limited numbers. At forks you must follow the arrow, or the fugitive pulls ahead, and a helicopter points out the route in levels 2, 4 and 5. Once you reach the fugitive, you ram him until he stops.

Good to know

On the arcade machine the clock rules the game: there are no lives, and the game ends when time runs out. A countdown from ten then lets you continue on the spot by inserting a coin. The factory setting gives 60 seconds to reach the fugitive, then as long again to stop him. Every yard driven earns 10 points. Overtaking a car is worth 200, then 400, and so on up to 8,000 with no crash; a collision resets the counter to 200. Each hit on the fugitive is worth the level number times 10,000. A cleared level pays its number times 100,000, plus 5,000 per second saved, and finishing the game awards 5,000,000. Continuing divides these bonuses by ten.

Its influence

Success called for sequels in arcades: Special Criminal Investigation in 1989, where the passenger can shoot through the roof, then Super Chase: Criminal Termination, and finally Chase H.Q. 2 in 2007. Two spin-offs followed, Crime City, an action shooter, and the quiz game Quiz H.Q. At Christmas 1989, Ocean Software's home computer conversions topped the UK sales chart. In Japan the game ranked tenth among the year's hit games at the 1989 Gamest Awards. Ray Tracers, in 1997, is described as a near follow-up, and the idea of ramming a vehicle while dodging traffic is cited as a forerunner of Driver and Burnout. The band Saint Etienne even recorded a demo inspired by the game, sampling its exclamations.
